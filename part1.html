Chapter One

An Introduction to Formal Privacy

1. Background and Motivation

1.1 Why Handling Personal Data Is Risky

Modern societies rely heavily on data. Large organizations routinely collect sensitive information about individuals, sometimes including details about our health, location, behavior, or finances. This data plays an important role in scientific research, public policy, and economic planning. At the same time, it creates serious privacy risks.

1.2 Trust Assumptions and Their Limits

This trust is usually based on several assumptions. We assume that the data is collected for a legitimate purpose, that the organization follows ethical guidelines, and that strong security practices are in place. However, these assumptions become much weaker once data leaves the organization that originally collected it.

"Privacy is not an option, and it shouldn't be the price we accept for just getting on the Internet."

1.3 When Data Is Shared

Data is often shared with third parties. Researchers may request access to study social or medical trends. Even when everyone involved acts in good faith, privacy risks remain. One major source of risk is inference attacks.

2. Reasoning About Privacy

2.1 A Simple Model

To reason about these risks, consider a simplified setting. Suppose a dataset contains one record per individual. Each record includes multiple attributes. Even if we remove explicit identifiers like names, the remaining attributes still describe real people.

2.2 Why Naive Anonymization Fails

2.2.1 The Myth of "Removing Identifiers"

A common intuition is that privacy can be achieved by deleting directly identifying fields. The problem is that identification rarely depends on a single attribute. ZIP code, age, gender—together, they can uniquely describe a person. These are known as quasi-identifiers.

D = (x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ)
Query Q ⊆ [n] → Answer = f(D_Q)

2.2.2 Cross-Referencing

Real-world datasets have repeatedly shown that removing explicit identifiers does not prevent re-identification when auxiliary information is available. One of the most powerful tools for re-identification is cross-referencing.

2.3 Limits of Query-Based Access

Carefully chosen queries can isolate individuals, especially when queries refer to small subpopulations. A common strategy is the differencing attack: asking two similar queries and subtracting their answers.
