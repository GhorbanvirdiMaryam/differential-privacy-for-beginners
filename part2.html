Reconstruction Attacks

3. Reconstruction Attacks

3.1 Reconstructing Private Data from Statistics

A reconstruction attack is one of the strongest ways to show that privacy has failed. In this type of attack, an adversary is able to recover the sensitive data of one or more people with very high accuracy. Sometimes the attacker can even recover the entire dataset.

The idea is simple: if the released numbers restrict the dataset enough, then only a few datasets—or even only one—can be consistent with them. When that happens, the attacker has effectively learned the original data.

3.1.1 A Classic Logic Puzzle: The Census Interview

Imagine a census worker visiting a home and asking:

“How many children do you have, and how old are they?”

The woman replies:

“I have three daughters. The product of their ages is 36.”

The census worker says: “That is not enough information.” The woman adds:

“The sum of their ages is the same as the house number next door.”

The census worker walks outside, checks the number, comes back, and says: “I still cannot determine their ages.” The woman then says:

“I have an oldest daughter.”

At that point, the census worker says: “Now I know all their ages.”

Step 1 — Using the product

Let the daughters’ ages be ( 0 ≤ a ≤ b ≤ c ). From the first answer:

a · b · c = 36.

The possible triples (a,b,c) that satisfy this are:

(1,1,36), (1,2,18), (1,3,12), (1,4,9), (1,6,6), (2,2,9), (2,3,6), (3,3,4).

Step 2 — Using the sum

The census taker checked the house number but still could not decide. That means the sum must not be unique. The only duplicated sum is:

a+b+c = 13,

which occurs for:

(1,6,6)     and     (2,2,9).

Step 3 — Using “oldest daughter”

The woman says she has an oldest daughter. That means the largest age is strictly greater than the others. So (1,6,6) is impossible. We must have:

(a,b,c) = (2,2,9).

3.1.2 Reconstructing from Aggregated Census Tables

Suppose we are told that there are three men in a block. Let their ages be (a ≤ b ≤ c). We are given two statistics:

  • The median age is 30
  • The mean age is 44
b = 30
(a + 30 + c) / 3 = 44   ⇒   a + c = 102.

This gives 30 possible age triples. Suppose we are then told that the mean age of two of the men is 28.

(a+b)/2 = 28   ⇒   (a+30)/2 = 28   ⇒   a=26.
c = 102 - 26 = 76.

The individual ages have been reconstructed exactly: (26, 30, 76).

Definition (Blatant Non-Privacy)

A system is blatantly non-private if there exists an attacker who, given the released outputs, can recover the underlying sensitive dataset with very high accuracy (for example, 99%).

A Formal Example of Complete Data Recovery
D = (x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ) ∈ {0,1}ⁿ

Each query selects a subset of people Q ⊆ [n]. The true answer is a(D,Q) = Σ_{i ∈ Q} xᵢ. To protect privacy, the system adds noise: ~a_Q = a(D,Q) + noise.

Theorem: Small Noise Allows Full Reconstruction

If the noise added to every query is at most n/400, then an attacker can reconstruct the dataset D with 99% accuracy.

How the attack works

Phase 1: The attacker asks all possible queries Q ⊆ [n].

Phase 2: The attacker searches for a dataset ~D ∈ {0,1}ⁿ that satisfies | ~a_Q - a(~D, Q) | ≤ n/400 for all Q.

Why this recovers the true data

By the triangle inequality, the attacker recovers at least 99% of all bits correctly:

| a(D,Q₀) - a(~D, Q₀) | ≤ 2n/400 = n/200.
"Releasing too many noisy statistics can completely destroy privacy."
